Work

What we do

Understanding & Diagnosis. We study how information, documents, and memory move through an organization, collection, or project. This includes the visible structures, such as catalogues, files, databases, inventories, and workflows, and the less visible ones: habits, decisions, gaps, dependencies, informal practices, and knowledge held by specific people. The aim is to understand what already exists, what is fragile, what is unclear, and what kind of structure is needed before any new system is designed.

System design & Repair. We create and repair the structures that allow knowledge to be organized, connected, and used. This work may include classification systems, cataloguing guidelines, metadata models, controlled vocabularies, thesauri, taxonomies, naming rules, folder structures, inventories, documentation templates, and semantic relationships. When needed, we also design more formal models for complex collections, research environments, or digital systems, as semantic relationships and design linked-data frameworks and ontologies, allowing information to connect across platforms, languages, institutions, and contexts.

Communication & Circulation. We turn organized knowledge into forms that can be read, shared, published, taught, searched, or explored. This may include digital libraries, websites, catalogues, dossiers, books, maps, educational materials, public documents, datasets, and other editorial or visual tools. The purpose is not simply to present information, but to make it accessible, meaningful, and usable.

Continuity & Stewardship. We design the practices that allow systems to remain alive after they are created. This includes workflows, governance structures, documentation routines, roles, responsibilities, training materials, maintenance procedures, and decision paths. The goal is autonomy: systems that can be understood, maintained, revised, and sustained by the people who use them.

Signs

When to call us

Tela de Araña is useful when knowledge, memory, or documentation have grown beyond the structures that once held them.

This may happen when archives, files, records, collections, databases, or publications are dispersed, inherited, unfinished, duplicated, or difficult to interpret.

It may also happen when folders, tags, categories, vocabularies, or metadata no longer describe the materials clearly; when a digital library, catalogue, repository, or publication system needs structure before it grows; or when an organization depends on knowledge that has never been properly documented.

We also work with projects where memory is fragile because it depends on specific people, informal practices, local vocabularies, temporary teams, or improvised workflows.

In all these cases, the question is not only how to organize information. It is how to create a structure that can respect context, remain usable, and continue to work over time.

Diagnosis

How work begins

Project-specific work begins with a diagnostic process.

The first contact should include a brief description of the materials, the current problem, the people or organization involved, the state of the existing structure, and the kind of support being considered. This allows us to determine whether Tela de Araña is the right place for the work.

If the project is a good fit, the next step is a paid diagnostic process. During this stage, we examine the current state of the materials, the structures already in place, the main risks, the points of fragility, and the possible forms of intervention.

The result is an initial orientation: what is happening, what is at risk, what has already been built, what should not be disturbed, and what kind of structure can responsibly support the work. From there, the project may continue in different forms: an advisory process, a vocabulary, a metadata model, a documentation plan, a workflow, a collection map, a governance structure, an implementation process, a deeper redesign, or another form of work defined by the diagnosis.

General notes and introductory tools may be shared publicly. Specific diagnosis, design, modeling, documentation, and implementation are professional work.

Small communities, grassroots organizations, local memory projects, cultural collectives, and fragile archives with limited access to paid professional support may also consult the Community Webs program below.

Principles

Our approach

We build from reality. Every archive, collection, organization, team, or community has its own history, materials, constraints, language, and rhythms. We begin there. Not with a platform, a trend, or a pre-packaged model, but with the actual conditions in which information is produced, used, remembered, and maintained.

We privilege coherence. A system can only work if its internal logic is clear. Before scale, automation, publication, or technical integration, there must be coherence: categories that can be understood, relationships that can be explained, decisions that can be traced, and structures that can be used without constant improvisation.

We prioritize practice. Tools must follow the work, not replace it. Technology, metadata, vocabularies, catalogues, platforms, and workflows are only useful when they support real practices and real needs. We choose or design them according to the logic of the materials and the people who use them.

We design for evolution. A system is not finished when it is delivered. It is finished only when it can be maintained, questioned, revised, and extended by the people who will live with it. For that reason, documentation, training, governance, and continuity are part of the design itself.

We focus on what holds. No noise, no gimmicks, no cosmetic innovation. Our work is structural: precise, rigorous, contextual, and built to last. We are interested in the parts of a system that allow knowledge and memory to remain usable when people, tools, priorities, and conditions change.

Method

The eight legs

1. Knowledge mapping

We document how information and memory actually behave: how they are produced, named, stored, circulated, used, accumulated, forgotten, or lost. This mapping reveals the system's flows, breaks, dependencies, risks, and opportunities, and provides the empirical basis for every later decision.

2. Memory reconstruction

We recover dispersed materials, reconnect fragmented archives, and reassemble documents, records, datasets, images, notes, and other traces so they can become usable again. The aim is not to erase disorder or difference, but to restore relation without flattening the history of the materials.

3. Structural design

We build the frameworks that organize knowledge and memory. This may include classification schemes, cataloguing guidelines, inventories, folder structures, collection maps, documentation systems, structural models, and navigational logics aligned with the real practices of the organization, collection, or community.

4. Semantic weaving

We design the structures that give language, meaning, and relation to a system. This includes controlled vocabularies, terminologies, taxonomies, metadata models, subject systems, multilingual labels, and semantic relationships that allow information to be described, connected, and found across languages, domains, and platforms.

5. Ontologies & formal modeling

For complex or interdisciplinary environments, we model entities, relationships, processes, events, places, people, objects, documents, and collections with formal semantic precision. This supports integration, advanced discovery, linked data, analytic reasoning, and systems that need to connect information across multiple contexts.

6. Editorial implementation & circulation

We translate structural decisions into communicative and functional outputs. This may include digital collections, catalogues, datasets, websites, dossiers, books, maps, educational materials, public documents, and other editorial or visual forms that allow organized knowledge to circulate. The focus is implementation: making the system visible, usable, and operational.

7. Documentation for the future

We embed documentation into the life of the system. This includes manuals, checklists, decision logs, update routines, workflow notes, naming rules, maintenance guides, and continuity practices that allow the system to remain coherent, current, and understandable over time. Documentation is not an accessory. It is part of the system's survival.

8. Governance & operational continuity

We define the stewardship structures that keep a system alive. This includes roles, responsibilities, maintenance procedures, policy frameworks, review cycles, decision paths, and criteria for change. The objective is long-term autonomy: systems that can be maintained, questioned, revised, and extended from within.

Public work

Community Webs

Tela de Araña offers part of its work free of charge to small communities, grassroots organizations, local memory projects, cultural collectives, and fragile archives whose knowledge and memory systems need structure.

Community Webs opens a free working space to look at materials, identify what is fragile or dispersed, and define practical first steps without separating knowledge from the language, decisions, and authority of those who hold it.

The work may begin with a conversation about what exists, what is dispersed, what is fragile, what is urgent, and what kind of structure could help. From there, the exchange may take different forms depending on the case: an initial map, a set of priorities, a basic documentation path, a vocabulary outline, a workflow, a small inventory model, or another practical form agreed together.

The purpose is to open a first structure where none exists, or to strengthen one that is already emerging, without replacing the community's own knowledge, language, decisions, or authority.

Community Webs is free, but not unlimited. Its scope is defined case by case, according to time, capacity, and the nature of the materials involved.

Name

Why Tela de Araña?

The name comes from minimal but powerful architecture — flexible, resilient, and built entirely through relation. It mirrors the systems of knowledge and memory we design.

It also carries a territorial resonance. In the world of the Muisca indigenous people of the altiplano cundiboyacense, spiderwebs were believed to be used to build the rafts that helped the dead cross a river in the afterlife. It is a reminder that in this region, crossings, threads, and woven forms have long been part of how meaning is carried.
